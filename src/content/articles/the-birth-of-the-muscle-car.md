In the early 1960s, General Motors had a policy that kept its mid-size cars from carrying engines larger than 330 cubic inches. It was meant to keep things sensible. A handful of people at Pontiac had other ideas.

## A small team with a big engine

Chief engineer **John Z. DeLorean**, together with engineers Bill Collins and Russ Gee and advertising man **Jim Wangers**, realized that the 389 V8 from Pontiac's full-size cars would bolt right into the new Tempest. Offering it as an *option package* rather than a new model was a clever way around the rule.

Pontiac general manager Elliot "Pete" Estes approved a limited run. Sales staff expected around 5,000 cars. Buyers took home more than 30,000 in the first year.

## The name

GTO stood for *Gran Turismo Omologato* — borrowed, a little cheekily, from Ferrari. Young buyers did not care about the Italian. They cared that a reasonably priced Pontiac could out-accelerate almost anything on the street.

## The ripple effect

Within a few years, every American maker had an answer:

- **Oldsmobile** 442
- **Chevrolet** Chevelle SS 396
- **Plymouth** Road Runner
- **Ford** Torino and Fairlane GT

By 1970, the muscle car was at its peak. Then insurance costs, emissions rules and the 1973 oil crisis brought the era to a close almost as quickly as it had begun.

## Why the story still matters

The muscle car was born from a simple idea: give ordinary people extraordinary performance at a fair price. More than sixty years later, that idea is still why so many of us lift the hood at car shows and smile.
